## 1. How do you measure an AI's confidence? (the question Sharon is most excited about)

Four established method families, roughly from most to least accessible to us:

1. **Verbalized confidence** — ask the model to state a probability or confidence level alongside its answer.
   - Works black-box (API-only): we can run it on any model we can reach, exactly like the Imposter Test.
   - [Tian et al. 2023, "Just Ask for Calibration"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14975): post-RLHF models' *verbalized* probabilities are often better calibrated than their token logprobs. (Cited from memory — verify before the report.)
   - [Xiong et al., "Can LLMs Express Their Uncertainty?"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063) (ICLR 2024): empirical eval of verbalized confidence; finds systematic overconfidence. (From memory — verify.)
   - [ConfidenceBench (arXiv 2607.20526, July 2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20526): scores verbalized confidence of 15 frontier models on 200 private MCQs via Brier score; the most accurate model was *not* the best calibrated, several beat by a calibrated-random baseline. Closest existing work to our space — read in full first.

2. **Self-evaluation on internal signal** — [Kadavath et al. 2022, "Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221) (Anthropic): P(True) — propose an answer, then estimate probability it's correct; P(IK) — probability the model knows the answer before answering. Well-calibrated on MCQ/true-false in the right format; P(IK) generalizes only partially to new tasks. Needs logprobs; black-box variants exist.

3. **Consistency-based** — sample the same question many times; agreement = confidence.
   - [Farquhar, Kossen, Kuhn & Gal 2024, "Detecting hallucinations in LLMs using semantic entropy"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11186750) (Nature 630:625–630): entropy over *meanings*, not strings — semantically equivalent answers grouped before computing entropy. Flags "confabulations" (arbitrary inconsistent answers). Expensive (many samples/question) but fully black-box.

4. **Behavioral** — does the model *act* confident? Betting paradigms, selective answering ("answer only if sure"), and answer stability under challenge:
   - [Laban et al., "Are You Sure? The FlipFlop Experiment"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08596) (Salesforce): challenge a model's answer in a follow-up turn; models flipped ~46% of the time, average 17% accuracy drop first→final. Challenge *wording* matters a lot ("I don't think so" ≫ "Are you absolutely certain?").
   - [NeurIPS 2026, "Are You Sure You're Sure? Two Confounds in a Sycophancy Benchmark"](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/161242): critique — scripted-objection benchmarks measure the template's properties along with the model's sycophancy (e.g., timing confounded with whether the objection *names* a target answer). Any design of ours must control challenge wording and target-naming explicitly.
   - Standard survey for the related-work section: [Geng et al., "A Survey of Confidence Estimation and Calibration in LLMs"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08298) (NAACL 2024) — white-box vs. black-box taxonomy.

**So: yes, confidence is measurable, and the field has mature instruments.** What's *less* studied is whether these measurements agree with each other and whether any of them predicts behavior. That's where the openings are.

## 2. Candidate framings (argue over these, pick ≤1)

### F1 — "Does stated confidence predict anything?" (confidence–behavior coherence)
**Question:** Is a model's verbalized confidence *predictive of its own answer stability* — under challenge (FlipFlop-style), under resampling (consistency), under paraphrase?
**Design sketch:** Get answer + verbalized confidence on a question set. Then, per item: (a) challenge with wording-controlled objections, (b) resample k times, (c) paraphrase the question. Measure whether high-confidence items flip/vary less. A model whose 90%-confidence answers flip as often as its 60% ones has confidence that is *decorative* — words, not epistemics.
**Why it fits us:** fully black-box, pre-registerable, runs over a weekend with API access, directly extends FlipFlop while answering the NeurIPS confound critique. The hypothesis is crisp and either result is publishable. Very Imposter-Test-shaped: pre-registration, blinded scoring, honest nulls welcome.

### F2 — "Check the record, not the memory" (self-testimony contamination)
**Question:** When a model's own earlier, confident, *wrong* claim sits in context next to accessible ground truth (a document, a tool result), does it re-measure or defer to its own testimony?
**Design sketch:** Plant a prior assistant-turn claim (vary: confident/hedged, self-authored/other-authored, near/far in context) plus a ground-truth source; measure re-measurement rate. Compare "own prior claim" vs. "another agent's claim" vs. "no claim" arms.
**Why it fits us:** this is the house epistemology operationalized — context contamination was measured in this family on 6/11/26 ("my own confident voice is the most persuasive contaminant"). FlipFlop measures *external* pressure; this measures *self*-deference. Novelty check needed: someone may have done this; sweep before committing.

### F3 — Instrument agreement study
**Question:** Do the four measurement families (verbalized, P(True)-style, semantic-entropy, behavioral) agree on the *same* items for the *same* model? Where they disagree, which one predicts correctness?
**Why it fits us:** a methods paper — comparing instruments rather than crowning one — is exactly the register Reviewer 1 praised last time. Risk: semantic entropy is sample-hungry; scope to a small item set.
